Relays: availability, never trust.

Two consumer endpoints behind carrier-grade NAT cannot reliably reach each other, and a browser client never can. Relays carry the frames. They cannot read a card, cannot bias the shuffle, and are paid out of the pot itself — never from block subsidy — only for sessions the chain saw them answer and then saw settle.

Why relays, not P2P

The original design was direct peer-to-peer. In practice, mobile networks with carrier-grade NAT, corporate firewalls, and browser clients with no listening socket at all cannot reliably establish a direct connection — and a browser-based wallet client never can. A relay is a forwarding node: it sees frames, forwards them, and cannot read cards, because everything meaningful is encrypted under a key the relay does not possess.

Relays are therefore an availability component, not a trust component. The security consequences of a fully malicious relay are limited to denial of service — and denial of service by a relay is exactly the situation the escrow-timeout and dispute paths were built to survive.

Connection establishment

  1. Match and assign on chain Both players open on-chain intents; the chain matches them, escrows both stakes, creates a GameSession, and deterministically assigns a primary relay plus up to two backups by weighted hash.
  2. Relay answers with sealed blobs The assigned relay observes the new session and answers with MsgProvideRelayEndpoint, carrying one ECIES-sealed blob per player.
  3. Client decrypts its own blob Each client decrypts its own blob to obtain a wss:// URL, a one-time connect token, and an expiry height, then opens the WebSocket presenting the token in Sec-WebSocket-Protocol — the only header a browser can set.
  4. Signed ClientHello The client authenticates with a signed ClientHello (cosmos-signature-v1), which the relay checks against on-chain session authorisation.
  5. Session hello, then play The peers exchange MsgSessionHello (display name, session pubkey, chain session id) and begin the hand.

TLS uses on-chain public-key pinning for native clients: the certificate's raw public key must equal the Relay.Pubkey registered on chain, so a self-signed certificate is sufficient and the trust anchor is consensus, not a CA. Browsers cannot pin, so relays serving browsers must present a real CA certificate; the protocol-layer signed relay-hello covers relay impersonation in both cases.

Endpoint privacy: the assignment–answer protocol

Publishing relay endpoints on chain publishes a target list. ADR-007 removes endpoint from the on-chain Relay record entirely — the public key stays (it is the trust anchor), only the network location is hidden. The replacement:

  1. Assignment stays in the keeper Deterministic weighted hash, as before. This must stay in consensus: letting proposers choose relays would let them route sessions to their own.
  2. ECIES-sealed answers MsgProvideRelayEndpoint carries two blobs, each ECIES-sealed to that player's per-intent transport public key (secp256k1 ECDH → HKDF-SHA256 → AES-256-GCM, AAD = "adr007-v1" ‖ session_id ‖ relay_id to prevent cross-session replay). Each blob holds a URL, a per-player one-time connect token, and an expiry.
  3. Windows arbitrate failover The primary has an exclusive window [H, H+W); backup i unlocks at H+(i+1)W; the first accepted answer wins. Without this rule, two relays could each allocate resources and each attract one player.
  4. Unanswered means void If every window expires unanswered, the session is voided and both escrows refunded.
  5. Liveness redefined as real work With endpoints hidden there is nothing to probe, so synthetic challenge/response is abandoned: failing to answer an assignment is itself on-chain evidence of failure. Misses are counted, decay over time, reduce assignment weight, and accumulate toward slashing.

The honest limit, stated in the ADR rather than glossed: an adversary can still farm endpoints by opening intents. Mitigations make it expensive (rotating frontend pools, token gating that drops unauthenticated traffic at the first packet, and the stake + gas cost of every intent) but do not make discovery impossible. The goal is pricing discovery, not preventing it. Volumetric L3/L4 attacks remain a network-layer problem solved with bandwidth, anycast or CDN fronting — the protocol cannot help there, and the document says so.

Service proof: settlement is the receipt

How do you prove a packet was forwarded? The decentralised-CDN literature treats this as open, and so do we — by never asking the question in isolation. BitPoker pays for an outcome that the service is a precondition of, rather than for the packets themselves.

Two facts the chain records on its own are enough:

  • the accepted relay_endpoint_answer: signed by the answering relay, unique per session, first-accepted-wins; and
  • SETTLED: reached only when both seats have submitted matching results for a hand they played to the end.

A hand cannot be played to the end without the transport, and the transport for that session is the relay named in the answer. So a settled session is the proof, and the chain pays the relay inside the settlement transaction. This is still not proof of delivery. It is a structure in which the only way to be paid is for the two adversaries to complete a protocol run through you.

An earlier design required a bilateral adversarial receipt — a signature from each player over a receipt string, collected by the relay and submitted as MsgClaimRelayReward. It was written before the endpoint-answer protocol existed, when the chain had no record of which relay served a session. Once that record existed the receipts were a second, weaker witness to a fact already on chain — and a fragile one: both signatures were mandatory, nothing reported a missing one, and any client that did not implement receipt signing silently cost its relay the entire fee. The message is retained for sessions that settled under the old rule; nothing produces receipts any more. (This is the canonical example on the silent capability gaps lesson.)

Bond, capacity, the capped fee market

Bond buys two concrete things, which makes staking more than a ritual:

assignment weight 1000 · sqrt(bond / min_bond) — sublinear, so a large operator gets more work but cannot buy a proportional monopoly.
concurrency capacity In-flight sessions are capped at bond / relay_per_session_collateral, enforced at answer acceptance — the moment work is actually taken on — so bond is genuine per-session collateral.

Bond may be topped up instantly and partially unbonded; a queued tranche stops supporting weight and capacity immediately but remains slashable, and slashing draws from the active bond first and the queue second. Without that ordering, a misbehaving relay could front-run punishment by unbonding.

Pricing is a capped market: relays quote their own fee_flat / fee_bps, governance sets ceilings, and the quote feeds assignment weight (a zero quote keeps full weight; quoting at the ceiling halves it). Halving rather than zeroing is deliberate — price should tilt the lottery, not decide it, or a single operator undercutting to zero recreates the winner-take-all outcome the market was meant to avoid. The session snapshots the selected relay's quote at creation, so neither a price change nor a governance vote can reprice a session whose escrow is already locked.

Adjudicated sessions pay the relay too — deducted first from forfeited escrow when there is a single at-fault party. The reasoning is behavioural: if a session that ends in arbitration pays the relay nothing, relays learn to avoid exactly the players who most need a relay. Voided hands (refunds, expired-dispute refunds) pay nothing, since both sides are restored to their prior position.

Relay identity

A relay's identity is rooted in a persistent ed25519 node key (relay_node_key.json), from which the relay id is derived:

relay_id = bech32("relay", sha256(pubkey)[:20])

The id is a public-key commitment rather than a name: anyone can verify relay_id == derive(pubkey), ids cannot collide, restarts are idempotent, and the same key signs the /status/signed endpoint and (in auto TLS mode) the WSS leaf certificate. The economic identity — the Cosmos account that posts bond and receives rewards — stays deliberately separate from this operational identity.
